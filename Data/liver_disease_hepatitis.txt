VIRAL HEPATITIS — Complete Clinical Guide (Hepatitis A, B, C, D, E)
Sources: WHO, CDC, AASLD, EASL Practice Guidelines
Reference: https://www.aasld.org/practice-guidelines

=============================================================
SECTION 1: OVERVIEW OF VIRAL HEPATITIS
=============================================================

Viral hepatitis is inflammation of the liver caused by hepatitis viruses. There are five main types:
- Hepatitis A (HAV): Fecal-oral transmission; acute, self-limiting
- Hepatitis B (HBV): Blood/sexual transmission; can be acute or chronic
- Hepatitis C (HCV): Blood transmission; usually chronic; curable with medications
- Hepatitis D (HDV): Only in HBV-infected patients; blood transmission
- Hepatitis E (HEV): Fecal-oral transmission; usually self-limiting; dangerous in pregnancy

GLOBAL BURDEN
- 296 million people live with chronic hepatitis B worldwide
- 58 million people live with chronic hepatitis C worldwide
- 1.1 million deaths annually from hepatitis B and C combined
- Leading causes of liver cirrhosis and liver cancer globally

=============================================================
SECTION 2: HEPATITIS A (HAV)
=============================================================

TRANSMISSION
- Fecal-oral route: Contaminated food, water, or close contact with infected person
- Common in areas with poor sanitation
- Shellfish (oysters, clams) from contaminated water
- International travel to endemic regions (South/Central America, Africa, South/Southeast Asia)

CLINICAL FEATURES
Incubation period: 15-50 days (average 28 days)
Acute illness symptoms:
- Sudden onset of fever, fatigue, nausea, vomiting
- Abdominal pain (right upper quadrant)
- Jaundice (yellow skin and eyes) — appears 1-2 weeks after initial symptoms
- Dark urine, pale/clay-colored stools
- Pruritus (itching)

Most infections are self-limiting; NO chronic form exists.
In children under 6: Often asymptomatic (only 30% develop jaundice)
In older adults: Typically more severe; risk of acute liver failure

DIAGNOSIS
- Anti-HAV IgM: Positive during acute infection
- Anti-HAV IgG: Indicates past infection or vaccination (immunity)
- Liver function tests: Elevated ALT/AST, bilirubin

TREATMENT
- Supportive care: Rest, adequate hydration, nutrition
- Avoid alcohol, acetaminophen, hepatotoxic drugs
- NO antiviral treatment needed
- Most patients recover fully within 2-3 months
- Rare cases (<1%): Acute liver failure — may need liver transplant

PREVENTION
- Hepatitis A vaccine: 2-dose series, highly effective (>94% efficacy)
  - Schedule: 0, 6-12 months
  - Recommended for all children aged 12-23 months
  - Recommended for travelers to endemic areas
- Good hand hygiene; safe food and water practices

SECTION 3: HEPATITIS B (HBV)
=============================================================

TRANSMISSION
- Sexual contact with infected person (most common in adults)
- Sharing needles/syringes (injection drug use)
- Mother to baby during birth (perinatal transmission — most common worldwide)
- Needlestick injuries in healthcare workers
- Sharing personal items: razors, toothbrushes
- NOT spread by: Hugging, coughing, sneezing, sharing food, breastfeeding

HBV is 50-100x more infectious than HIV.

CLINICAL PHASES
Acute Hepatitis B (first 6 months):
- Many asymptomatic (especially adults)
- Symptomatic: Fatigue, nausea, jaundice, RUQ pain
- 95% of immunocompetent adults clear the infection spontaneously
- 5% develop chronic infection

Chronic Hepatitis B (>6 months infection):
HBeAg-positive (immune tolerant/active phase):
- High HBV DNA levels, minimal liver damage initially
- Common in perinatally infected patients

HBeAg-negative (immune active/inactive phases):
- May have fluctuating ALT, liver inflammation continuing
- Pre-core mutants maintain viral replication

SEROLOGICAL MARKERS
| Marker | Meaning |
|--------|---------|
| HBsAg | Surface antigen — indicates active infection (acute or chronic) |
| Anti-HBs | Surface antibody — indicates recovery or vaccination (immunity) |
| HBcAb IgM | Core IgM — acute hepatitis B |
| HBcAb IgG | Core IgG — past or chronic infection |
| HBeAg | e-antigen — high viral replication, highly infectious |
| Anti-HBe | e-antibody — lower replication |
| HBV DNA | Viral load — quantifies replication level |
| HBV genotype | A-I — affects treatment response and disease course |

DIAGNOSIS
- HBsAg positive for >6 months = Chronic HBV
- HBV DNA levels: Indicate viral replication level
  - High (>2000 IU/mL with liver damage): Treatment recommended
- ALT: Elevated with active hepatocellular inflammation
- Liver biopsy or FibroScan: Assess fibrosis stage
- Screen for HCC: Ultrasound + AFP every 6 months (in cirrhosis or high-risk patients)

TREATMENT
Indications for treatment:
- HBV DNA >2000 IU/mL + elevated ALT (>2x ULN) OR significant fibrosis (≥F2)
- Any cirrhotic patient with detectable HBV DNA
- Immunosuppressed patients receiving chemotherapy

Antiviral Medications (Nucleoside/Nucleotide Analogues — NAs):
Preferred first-line:
- Tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (TDF/Viread): 300mg daily — most potent, no resistance
- Tenofovir alafenamide (TAF/Vemlidy): 25mg daily — better renal/bone safety profile
- Entecavir (Baraclude): 0.5mg daily (1mg in lamivudine-experienced) — excellent resistance barrier

Interferon-based:
- Pegylated interferon-alpha 2a (Pegasys): 48-week course; finite duration but side effects
- Benefits: Finite duration, off-treatment immune control; Drawbacks: Significant side effects

Treatment goals:
- HBsAg loss (functional cure) — rare but possible
- Sustained HBV DNA suppression — prevents cirrhosis and HCC
- HBeAg seroconversion (HBeAg-positive patients)
- Normalization of ALT
- Regression of fibrosis

Duration: Usually lifelong in HBeAg-negative patients; HBeAg-positive patients may stop after HBeAg seroconversion + 12 additional months

COMPLICATIONS
- Cirrhosis: 20-30% of chronic HBV develop cirrhosis over 25 years
- Hepatocellular Carcinoma: Risk even without cirrhosis in HBV
- Reactivation: Can occur with immunosuppression (chemotherapy, biologics)

PREVENTION
- HBV Vaccine: 3-dose series (0, 1, 6 months) — 90-95% effective
  - Recommended for all infants (first dose within 24 hours of birth)
  - Recommended for all unvaccinated adults
- Post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP): HBV vaccine + HBIG within 24 hours of exposure
- Hepatitis B immune globulin (HBIG): For newborns of HBsAg-positive mothers at birth

SECTION 4: HEPATITIS C (HCV)
=============================================================

TRANSMISSION
- Primary route: Sharing needles, syringes, or drug paraphernalia (injection drug use)
- Blood transfusions/organ transplants (before 1992 screening implementation)
- Needlestick injuries in healthcare settings
- Less efficiently: Sexual contact (higher risk with HIV coinfection, multiple partners)
- Perinatal (mother to baby): ~5-6% risk; less common than HBV
- NOT spread by: Casual contact, hugging, kissing, sharing food, breastfeeding

CLINICAL FEATURES
Incubation period: 2-12 weeks (average 6-7 weeks)

Acute HCV (first 6 months):
- 70-80% have NO symptoms (subclinical)
- When symptomatic: Fatigue, nausea, jaundice (mild)
- 25-30% clear the virus spontaneously

Chronic HCV (>6 months after infection):
- Develops in 70-75% of acutely infected patients
- Most have few or no symptoms for decades ("silent epidemic")
- Gradual liver damage over 20-30 years
- 20-30% develop cirrhosis

HCV GENOTYPES
| Genotype | Geographic Prevalence |
|----------|----------------------|
| GT1 (1a, 1b) | North America, Europe — most common globally |
| GT2 | Europe, North/West Africa |
| GT3 | South Asia, Southeast Asia, Australia |
| GT4 | Middle East, Central Africa, Egypt |
| GT5 | South Africa |
| GT6 | Southeast Asia |

Genotype affects treatment selection but with newer DAAs, pan-genotypic options available.

DIAGNOSIS
- Anti-HCV antibody (ELISA): Screening test; becomes positive 8-12 weeks after infection
- HCV RNA (PCR): Confirms active infection; quantifies viral load
- HCV genotype: Determines specific treatment regimen
- Liver fibrosis assessment: FibroScan, FIB-4, liver biopsy

HCV TREATMENT — Direct-Acting Antivirals (DAAs)
Revolutionary advance: HCV is now CURABLE in >95% of patients.

Mechanism: DAAs target specific HCV proteins:
- NS5B polymerase inhibitors: Sofosbuvir (Sovaldi) — backbone of most regimens
- NS5A inhibitors: Ledipasvir, velpatasvir, pibrentasvir, elbasvir — potent antiviral activity
- NS3/4A protease inhibitors: Glecaprevir, grazoprevir, simeprevir — can't use in decompensated cirrhosis

Preferred Pan-Genotypic Regimens (work for all genotypes):
1. Sofosbuvir/Velpatasvir (Epclusa):
   - 400/100mg once daily × 12 weeks
   - Pan-genotypic; SVR rates >95%
   - Can add ribavirin for decompensated cirrhosis

2. Glecaprevir/Pibrentasvir (Mavyret):
   - 300/120mg once daily × 8 weeks (no cirrhosis or compensated cirrhosis)
   - Pan-genotypic; SVR rates >97-99%
   - NOT for decompensated cirrhosis

3. Sofosbuvir/Ledipasvir (Harvoni): Genotype 1, 4, 5, 6 — 12 weeks

SVR (Sustained Virological Response) = CURE:
- HCV RNA undetectable 12 weeks after completing treatment
- SVR12 = cure rate; prevents cirrhosis, reverses early fibrosis, dramatically reduces HCC risk

WHO CAN BE TREATED?
All patients with chronic HCV should be offered treatment regardless of:
- Fibrosis stage
- Age
- Symptoms
- Prior treatment history

Special populations:
- Decompensated cirrhosis: Sofosbuvir/velpatasvir ± ribavirin (avoid protease inhibitors)
- CKD/dialysis: Glecaprevir/pibrentasvir (sofosbuvir dose adjustment needed)
- HIV coinfection: Check drug interactions; same efficacy rates
- After liver transplant: Effective; prevents graft re-infection

HEPATITIS C AND LIVER CANCER
- HCV is a leading cause of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC)
- Even after SVR (cure), cirrhotic patients need continued HCC surveillance
- SVR reduces HCC risk by 70-80% but does not eliminate it in cirrhosis

HEPATITIS C PREVENTION
- No vaccine available (unlike Hepatitis A and B)
- Prevention: Avoid sharing needles/drug equipment; use clean needles
- Harm reduction programs: Needle exchange programs
- Healthcare settings: Standard precautions, proper needle disposal
- Pre-exposure prophylaxis: Being researched; no current standard recommendation

SECTION 5: HEPATITIS D (HDV) / DELTA HEPATITIS
=============================================================

KEY FACTS
- Hepatitis D is a "satellite" virus — it CANNOT infect alone; REQUIRES HBV surface antigen (HBsAg)
- Only HBV-infected individuals can get hepatitis D
- Affects an estimated 15-20 million people with HBV worldwide
- More severe disease than HBV alone

TYPES OF INFECTION
1. Coinfection: Simultaneous HBV + HDV infection
   - Usually acute, self-limiting (clears with HBV)
   - Risk of acute liver failure higher than HBV alone
   - 95% of immunocompetent adults clear both viruses

2. Superinfection: HDV infection in a person with existing chronic HBV
   - More severe prognosis
   - 70-90% develop chronic hepatitis D
   - Rapidly accelerates liver disease — cirrhosis develops in 70% within 5-10 years

DIAGNOSIS
- Anti-HDV IgM: Acute HDV
- Anti-HDV IgG: Past/chronic HDV
- HDV RNA PCR: Confirms active replication

TREATMENT
- HDV is difficult to treat
- Pegylated interferon-alpha: 48-72 weeks; only partially effective
- Bulevirtide (Hepcludex): First approved drug for HDV in Europe (2020); Entry inhibitor
  - 2mg subcutaneous injection daily; shows significant HDV RNA suppression

PREVENTION
- Prevention of HBV = prevention of HDV
- HBV vaccination fully protects against HDV
- Already HBV-infected: Avoid HDV exposure (safe sex, no shared needles)

SECTION 6: HEPATITIS E (HEV)
=============================================================

KEY FACTS
- Fecal-oral transmission (like HAV) via contaminated water
- Mainly affects developing countries with poor sanitation
- 4 main genotypes: GT1/2 (humans only — developing countries); GT3/4 (zoonotic — pigs, deer)
- GT3 increasingly recognized in developed countries (through undercooked pork)

CLINICAL FEATURES
Incubation: 2-10 weeks

Usually self-limiting (like HAV):
- Fever, malaise, jaundice, anorexia, nausea
- Resolves in 4-6 weeks in most patients

SPECIAL POPULATIONS
Pregnant women (especially 3rd trimester):
- Most severe disease; up to 25% mortality from acute liver failure
- Highest risk for fulminant hepatic failure
- Can cause fetal loss and neonatal death

Immunocompromised patients (GT3/4):
- Organ transplant recipients, HIV patients
- Can develop CHRONIC hepatitis E (unique — not seen with GT1/2)
- Requires antiviral treatment

DIAGNOSIS
- Anti-HEV IgM: Acute infection
- HEV RNA PCR: In immunocompromised patients (antibody tests less reliable)

TREATMENT
- Immunocompetent patients: Supportive care; self-resolves
- Chronic HEV (immunocompromised): Reduction of immunosuppression; Ribavirin (12-24 weeks)
- Pegylated interferon-alpha: Used in some transplant patients

PREVENTION
- Clean water and sanitation
- Avoid undercooked pork and game meats (GT3/4)
- HEV vaccine (HEV 239/Hecolin): Licensed in China; not yet globally available
- Pregnant women: Avoid travel to endemic areas; ensure safe drinking water

SECTION 7: HEPATITIS AND LIVER CANCER
=============================================================

HEPATOCELLULAR CARCINOMA (HCC) RISK BY ETIOLOGY
- Chronic HBV without cirrhosis: Annual HCC risk ~0.1-0.4%
- Chronic HBV with cirrhosis: Annual HCC risk ~2-4%
- Chronic HCV with cirrhosis: Annual HCC risk ~2-4%
- HBV + HCV coinfection: Highest risk, synergistic effect

HCC SURVEILLANCE RECOMMENDATIONS (AASLD/EASL)
All cirrhotic patients: Ultrasound + AFP every 6 months
HBV-specific surveillance (even without cirrhosis):
- Asian men >40 years old: Start surveillance
- Asian women >50 years old: Start surveillance
- Any HBV patient with family history of HCC

TREATMENT IMPACT ON HCC RISK
- HBV treatment (nucleotide analogues): Reduces HCC risk by 50-70% in cirrhosis
- HCV cure (SVR): Reduces HCC risk by 70-80% (does NOT eliminate in cirrhosis)
- HBV vaccine: Highly effective primary prevention; reduced HCC by 70% in Taiwan cohort
