=============================================================
SECTION 1: INTRODUCTION TO CHRONIC LIVER DISEASE (CLD)
=============================================================

Chronic liver disease (CLD) is a progressive deterioration of liver functions for more than six months, which includes synthesis of clotting factors, other proteins, detoxification of harmful products of metabolism, and excretion of bile. CLD is a continuous process of inflammation, destruction, and regeneration of liver parenchyma, which leads to fibrosis and cirrhosis.

The spectrum of etiologies is broad for chronic liver disease, which includes toxins, alcohol abuse for a prolonged time, infection, autoimmune diseases, genetic and metabolic disorders. Cirrhosis is the final stage of chronic liver disease that results in disruption of liver architecture, the formation of widespread nodules, vascular reorganization, neo-angiogenesis, and deposition of an extracellular matrix.

SECTION 2: ETIOLOGY (CAUSES OF LIVER DISEASE)
=============================================================

ALCOHOLIC LIVER DISEASE (ALD)
Alcoholic liver disease is a spectrum of disease which includes:
- Alcoholic fatty liver (steatosis) — reversible with alcohol abstinence
- Alcoholic hepatitis — can be acute or chronic, partially reversible
- Alcoholic cirrhosis — irreversible, end-stage
Patients with severe alcohol use disorder mostly develop chronic liver disease; this is the most frequent cause of CLD in the developed world.
AST:ALT ratio is typically 2:1 or greater in alcoholic liver disease.
Treatment: Complete alcohol abstinence, nutritional support, corticosteroids (in severe cases), liver transplant.

NON-ALCOHOLIC FATTY LIVER DISEASE (NAFLD / MASLD)
NAFLD has an association with metabolic syndrome (obesity, hyperlipidemia, and diabetes mellitus).
- NAFLD: Fat accumulation in the liver without significant inflammation
- NASH (Non-alcoholic steatohepatitis): Fat + inflammation + liver cell damage → can lead to fibrosis
- MASLD (Metabolic-Associated Steatotic Liver Disease): New 2023 nomenclature replacing NAFLD
Risk factors: Obesity (BMI >30), type 2 diabetes, hypertriglyceridemia, hypertension, insulin resistance
Diagnosis: ALT > AST pattern, ultrasound showing echogenic (bright) liver, liver biopsy for staging
Treatment: Weight loss (>7-10% body weight), exercise, diabetes control, Mediterranean diet, resmetirom (Rezdiffra — FDA approved 2024 for NASH with fibrosis)

CHRONIC VIRAL HEPATITIS
Chronic hepatitis B, C, and D infections are the most common causes of chronic liver disease in East Asia and Sub-Saharan Africa.
- Hepatitis C genotype 1a and 1b: Most prevalent in Europe and North America
- Hepatitis C genotype 3: More common in Southeast Asia
- Chronic hepatitis C, if not treated, may lead to hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC)

GENETIC CAUSES
Alpha-1 Antitrypsin Deficiency:
- Most common genetic cause of CLD among children
- Leads to liver cirrhosis and lung emphysema
- Treatment: Alpha-1 augmentation therapy, liver transplant

Hereditary Hemochromatosis:
- Autosomal recessive disorder of iron absorption
- Mutation in HFE gene (C282Y mutation) that regulates iron absorption
- Leads to excessive iron deposition in liver, heart, pancreas, joints
- Lab: Raised serum iron, raised ferritin, decreased TIBC
- Treatment: Phlebotomy (regular blood removal), iron chelators (deferoxamine)

Wilson Disease:
- Autosomal recessive disorder leading to copper accumulation
- Mutation in ATP7B gene causing impaired copper excretion
- Lab: Raised urine copper, decreased serum ceruloplasmin, Kayser-Fleischer rings in eyes
- Treatment: Copper chelators (penicillamine, trientine), zinc acetate, liver transplant

AUTOIMMUNE CAUSES
Autoimmune Hepatitis (AIH):
- Destruction of liver parenchyma by autoantibodies
- More common in females
- Lab: Elevated ANA, anti-smooth muscle antibodies (ASMA), LKM-1, hypergammaglobulinemia
- Treatment: Corticosteroids (prednisone), azathioprine

Primary Biliary Cholangitis (PBC):
- Autoimmune destruction of intrahepatic bile ducts
- More common in middle-aged women
- Lab: Markedly elevated alkaline phosphatase (ALP), positive antimitochondrial antibodies (AMA)
- Treatment: Ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA), obeticholic acid

Primary Sclerosing Cholangitis (PSC):
- Inflammation and fibrosis of intrahepatic and extrahepatic bile ducts
- Commonly associated with ulcerative colitis (IBD)
- Treatment: ERCP, biliary stenting, liver transplant

DRUG-INDUCED LIVER INJURY (DILI)
Common culprit drugs:
- Amiodarone (antiarrhythmic)
- Isoniazid (tuberculosis treatment)
- Methotrexate (rheumatoid arthritis, psoriasis)
- Phenytoin (anticonvulsant)
- Nitrofurantoin (antibiotic)
- Acetaminophen/Paracetamol: Dose-dependent hepatotoxicity; most common cause of acute liver failure in the US
Treatment: Identify and stop the causative drug; N-acetylcysteine (NAC) for acetaminophen overdose

SECTION 3: STAGING OF LIVER DISEASE
=============================================================

The four stages of liver disease progression:
1. Hepatitis / Steatosis / Hepatosteatosis — Liver inflammation or fat accumulation
2. Fibrosis — Scar tissue formation (F0-F4 on METAVIR scale)
3. Cirrhosis — Advanced scarring, distorted liver architecture (irreversible)
4. Hepatocellular Carcinoma (HCC) — Liver cancer arising from cirrhosis

METAVIR Fibrosis Score (liver biopsy):
- F0: No fibrosis
- F1: Portal fibrosis without septa
- F2: Portal fibrosis with rare septa
- F3: Numerous septa without cirrhosis
- F4: Cirrhosis

Child-Pugh Score (assessing severity of cirrhosis):
Uses: Ascites, Bilirubin, Albumin, PT/INR, Hepatic Encephalopathy
- Class A (score 5-6): Well-compensated disease — good prognosis
- Class B (score 7-9): Functional compromise — moderate prognosis
- Class C (score 10-15): Decompensated disease — poor prognosis

MELD Score (Model for End-Stage Liver Disease):
Uses: Serum bilirubin, serum creatinine, INR
- Used to prioritize patients for liver transplant
- MELD score ≥21 indicates poor prognosis (mean survival ~6 months)
- Higher MELD = greater urgency for liver transplant

SECTION 4: CLINICAL MANIFESTATIONS (SIGNS & SYMPTOMS)
=============================================================

GENERAL / NON-SPECIFIC SYMPTOMS
- Fatigue and weakness
- Loss of appetite (anorexia)
- Nausea and vomiting
- Unintentional weight loss
- Right upper quadrant (RUQ) discomfort or pain

PORTAL HYPERTENSION
Portal venous pressure > 7 mmHg = portal hypertension; clinical complications develop at >12 mmHg.

Consequences of Portal Hypertension:
1. Esophageal Varices: Dilated veins in the esophagus. Present with hematemesis (vomiting blood) or melena (dark tarry stools). Most life-threatening complication.
2. Ascites: Fluid accumulation in the peritoneal cavity. Clinical signs: abdominal distension, shifting dullness, fluid wave.
3. Caput Medusae: Dilated veins around the navel (umbilicus)
4. Splenomegaly: Enlarged spleen leading to thrombocytopenia (low platelets)
5. Rectal hemorrhoids

HEPATOCELLULAR INSUFFICIENCY
Jaundice:
- Yellowing of skin, eyes (scleral icterus), and mucous membranes
- Due to elevated bilirubin (total bilirubin >2 mg/dL makes jaundice visible)
- Urine becomes dark (tea-colored), stools become pale
- Pruritus (itching) due to bile salt accumulation

Hepatic Encephalopathy (HE):
- Neuropsychiatric syndrome from liver's failure to detoxify ammonia
- Grades:
  - Grade 0/Minimal: Subclinical, normal mental status
  - Grade 1: Mild confusion, sleep disturbance, shortened attention span
  - Grade 2: Lethargy, disorientation to time, asterixis (flapping tremor)
  - Grade 3: Somnolence, gross disorientation, bizarre behavior
  - Grade 4: Coma
- Precipitating factors: GI bleeding, infection, constipation, sedatives, electrolyte imbalance

Coagulopathy:
- Liver produces clotting factors (I, II, V, VII, IX, X, XI)
- In CLD: prolonged PT/INR, easy bruising, bleeding tendency

Hypoalbuminemia:
- Reduced protein synthesis → low serum albumin → ascites, peripheral edema

SIGNS OF CHRONIC LIVER DISEASE (Hyperestrinism)
Estrogen buildup due to impaired catabolism:
- Spider angiomas (spider nevi): Dilated arterioles with a central red spot and radiating legs
- Palmar erythema: Redness of the palms
- Gynecomastia: Enlarged breast tissue in men
- Testicular atrophy
- Leukonychia: White nails (Terry's nails)
- Dupuytren's contracture: Thickening of palm fascia
- Clubbing of fingers

HEPATORENAL SYNDROME (HRS)
- Functional renal failure in patients with advanced liver disease
- HRS-1: Rapid progressive (creatinine doubles within 2 weeks) — high mortality
- HRS-2: Slower progression, associated with refractory ascites
- Diagnosis: Rising creatinine, oliguria, urine sodium <10 meq/L, no response to volume expansion

SPONTANEOUS BACTERIAL PERITONITIS (SBP)
- Bacterial infection of ascitic fluid
- Common organisms: E. coli, Klebsiella, Streptococcus pneumoniae
- Diagnosis: Ascitic fluid PMN >250 cells/μL
- Treatment: IV cefotaxime (3rd gen cephalosporin), albumin infusion

SECTION 5: LABORATORY FINDINGS
=============================================================

LIVER FUNCTION TESTS (LFTs)
- ALT (Alanine Aminotransferase): Elevated in hepatocellular damage; more liver-specific than AST
- AST (Aspartate Aminotransferase): Elevated in hepatocellular damage; also elevated in muscle/cardiac injury
- AST:ALT ratio >2 suggests alcoholic liver disease
- ALP (Alkaline Phosphatase): Elevated in cholestatic disease (PBC, PSC, bile duct obstruction)
- GGT (Gamma-glutamyltransferase): Elevated in cholestatic disease and alcohol use
- Bilirubin (Total, Direct, Indirect): Elevated in liver dysfunction and jaundice
- Albumin: Decreased in chronic liver disease (marker of synthetic function)
- PT/INR: Prolonged in liver disease (liver makes clotting factors)
- Ammonia: Elevated in hepatic encephalopathy

SPECIFIC MARKERS
- Hepatitis B: HBsAg, HBeAg, anti-HBs, anti-HBc, HBV DNA viral load
- Hepatitis C: Anti-HCV antibody, HCV RNA PCR, HCV genotype
- Hemochromatosis: Serum ferritin, serum iron, transferrin saturation, HFE gene mutation
- Wilson disease: Serum ceruloplasmin (decreased), 24-hour urine copper (increased)
- Autoimmune hepatitis: ANA, ASMA, anti-LKM-1
- PBC: Antimitochondrial antibodies (AMA-M2)
- AFP (Alpha-fetoprotein): Tumor marker for hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC)

SERUM ASCITES ALBUMIN GRADIENT (SAAG)
SAAG = Serum albumin - Ascitic fluid albumin
- SAAG ≥1.1 g/dL: Portal hypertension (cirrhosis, Budd-Chiari syndrome, right heart failure)
- SAAG <1.1 g/dL: Non-portal hypertension causes (malignancy, TB peritonitis, nephrotic syndrome)

SECTION 6: RADIOLOGY / IMAGING
=============================================================

ULTRASOUND ABDOMEN
- Most affordable and first-line imaging for liver disease
- Detects: Liver size, echogenicity, nodularity, ascites, splenomegaly
- Portal vein diameter >13 mm suggests portal hypertension
- Can detect hepatocellular carcinoma lesions

CT SCAN (Computed Tomography)
- More detailed assessment of liver lesions
- Triphasic CT (arterial + portal venous + delayed phases): Gold standard for HCC diagnosis
- HCC shows arterial enhancement + washout in portal/delayed phase

TRANSIENT ELASTOGRAPHY (FIBROSCAN)
- Non-invasive method to measure liver stiffness (fibrosis)
- Liver stiffness measurement (LSM) in kPa correlates with METAVIR fibrosis stage
- F0-F1: <7 kPa; F2: 7-9 kPa; F3: 9-12 kPa; F4 (cirrhosis): >12-15 kPa
- Recommended by EASL as most effective non-invasive approach to diagnose cirrhosis

MRI (Magnetic Resonance Imaging)
- Superior soft-tissue resolution
- MRI with gadoxetic acid: Excellent for detecting small HCC lesions
- MRCP: Non-invasive evaluation of bile ducts (PSC, biliary obstruction)

LIVER BIOPSY
- Gold standard for staging fibrosis and diagnosing specific liver diseases
- Techniques: Percutaneous, transjugular (in coagulopathy), laparoscopic
- Provides METAVIR or Ishak fibrosis scoring

ENDOSCOPY (EGD — Esophagogastroduodenoscopy)
- Diagnoses esophageal varices and determines their size
- Small varices: <5 mm; Large varices: >5 mm
- Allows treatment with variceal banding or sclerotherapy

SECTION 7: COMPLICATIONS OF CHRONIC LIVER DISEASE
=============================================================

1. Variceal Bleeding (most life-threatening acute complication)
2. Ascites and Spontaneous Bacterial Peritonitis (SBP)
3. Hepatic Encephalopathy
4. Hepatorenal Syndrome (HRS)
5. Hepatopulmonary Syndrome (HPS): Dilated pulmonary vessels causing hypoxia
6. Hepatocellular Carcinoma (HCC): Annual risk 2-4% in cirrhotic patients
7. Coagulopathy and Bleeding
8. Malnutrition and Muscle Wasting (sarcopenia)

HEPATOCELLULAR CARCINOMA (HCC)
- Most common primary liver cancer
- Risk factors: Cirrhosis (any cause), chronic HBV, chronic HCV, alcohol, NASH, aflatoxin exposure
- Surveillance: Ultrasound + AFP every 6 months in all cirrhotic patients
- Diagnosis: Triphasic CT or MRI (arterial enhancement + washout)
- Staging: Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) system

SECTION 8: TREATMENT PROTOCOLS
=============================================================

GENERAL MANAGEMENT
Esophageal Varices:
- Acute bleed: IV octreotide/terlipressin, broad-spectrum antibiotics, endoscopic band ligation
- Prophylaxis: Non-selective beta-blockers (propranolol, nadolol, carvedilol)
- TIPS (Transjugular Intrahepatic Portosystemic Shunt): For refractory varices or ascites
- Primary prophylaxis: EGD screening every 2-3 years in compensated cirrhosis

Ascites:
- Low-sodium diet (<2g sodium/day)
- Diuretics: Spironolactone (aldosterone antagonist) + Furosemide (loop diuretic)
- Tense ascites: Therapeutic paracentesis + IV albumin infusion (8g/L of fluid removed)
- Refractory ascites: Repeated paracentesis, TIPS, liver transplant evaluation

Hepatic Encephalopathy:
- Identify and treat precipitating factors (GI bleed, infection, electrolyte imbalance)
- Lactulose: Converts ammonia to ammonium, reduces GI absorption (titrate to 2-3 soft stools/day)
- Rifaximin: Antibiotic that reduces ammonia-producing gut bacteria (550mg twice daily)
- Dietary protein: Adequate protein intake (1.2-1.5g/kg/day); vegetable protein preferred

Hepatorenal Syndrome:
- HRS-1: Terlipressin + albumin infusion (first-line in many countries); norepinephrine + albumin
- Midodrine + octreotide + albumin: Alternative where terlipressin unavailable
- Liver transplant: Only definitive cure

SPECIFIC DISEASE TREATMENTS
- Alcoholic Liver Disease: Complete abstinence from alcohol; prednisolone for severe alcoholic hepatitis
- NAFLD/NASH: Weight loss, exercise, control of metabolic syndrome; resmetirom (Rezdiffra) for F2-F3
- Hepatitis B: Tenofovir disoproxil (TDF), tenofovir alafenamide (TAF), entecavir — long-term suppression
- Hepatitis C: Direct-acting antivirals (DAAs) — sofosbuvir/ledipasvir, glecaprevir/pibrentasvir — 8-12 week cure
- Autoimmune Hepatitis: Prednisolone + azathioprine (maintenance)
- PBC: Ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA) 13-15 mg/kg/day; obeticholic acid for inadequate UDCA response
- PSC: Endoscopic management, liver transplant
- Hemochromatosis: Phlebotomy (weekly until ferritin <50 μg/L), then maintenance phlebotomy
- Wilson Disease: Penicillamine, trientine, zinc acetate; liver transplant for liver failure
- Alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency: Supportive; liver transplant (curative)
- Budd-Chiari syndrome: Anticoagulation, thrombolysis, angioplasty/stenting, TIPS, liver transplant

LIVER TRANSPLANT
Indications:
- Decompensated cirrhosis (MELD ≥15)
- Acute liver failure
- HCC within Milan criteria (1 lesion ≤5cm OR ≤3 lesions each ≤3cm)
- Metabolic liver diseases (Wilson disease, A1AT deficiency)
Contraindications: Active extrahepatic malignancy, severe cardiopulmonary disease, active substance abuse

SECTION 9: PREVENTION & PATIENT EDUCATION
=============================================================

PREVENTIVE MEASURES
- Avoid all forms of alcohol (wine, beer, spirits, mixed drinks)
- Get vaccinated against Hepatitis A and Hepatitis B
- Practice safe sex and avoid sharing needles (prevent hepatitis B and C)
- Maintain healthy weight and exercise regularly (prevent NAFLD)
- Control diabetes, hypertension, and high cholesterol (metabolic syndrome)
- Avoid unnecessary medications; never combine alcohol with acetaminophen
- Avoid iron supplements unless prescribed (risk in hemochromatosis)
- Do not take herbal supplements without doctor approval (many are hepatotoxic)
- Regular screening if at risk for hepatitis B/C

DIET FOR LIVER DISEASE PATIENTS
General principles:
- Low sodium diet (<2g/day) to prevent/control ascites
- Adequate protein (1.2-1.5g/kg/day); do NOT restrict protein unless essential
- Avoid raw shellfish (risk of Vibrio vulnificus infection in cirrhotic patients)
- Small frequent meals; nighttime snack to prevent fasting catabolism
- Mediterranean diet: Rich in fruits, vegetables, whole grains, olive oil, fish
- Avoid saturated fats, processed foods, sugary beverages
- Vitamin E 800 IU/day may benefit non-diabetic NASH patients

MONITORING & SURVEILLANCE
Routine (all CLD patients):
- Blood tests every 3-6 months: CBC, LFTs, CMP, PT/INR
- Abdominal ultrasound every 6 months (HCC surveillance in cirrhosis)
- Upper endoscopy (EGD): Every 2-3 years for variceal surveillance
- Annual assessment of renal function

SECTION 10: PROGNOSIS & SCORING SYSTEMS
=============================================================

COMPENSATED vs DECOMPENSATED CIRRHOSIS
- Compensated cirrhosis: No major complications; median survival >10 years
- Decompensated cirrhosis: Variceal bleed, ascites, HCC, SBP, hepatorenal syndrome
  - Mean survival ~6 months if Child-Pugh ≥12 or MELD ≥21

FIBROSIS REGRESSION
While fibrosis is generally considered irreversible, early-stage fibrosis (F1-F2) can regress with:
- Sustained virological response (SVR) in hepatitis C (effectively a cure)
- Alcohol abstinence in alcoholic liver disease
- Significant weight loss in NAFLD

Rate of fibrosis progression:
- Fastest: HIV-HCV coinfection
- Slowest: Primary biliary cholangitis
- Higher age, male sex, obesity, alcohol use all accelerate fibrosis progression
