LIVER DISEASE TREATMENTS & AASLD CLINICAL GUIDELINES
Sources: AASLD Practice Guidelines, EASL Clinical Practice Guidelines, UpToDate
Reference: https://www.aasld.org/practice-guidelines

=============================================================
SECTION 1: OVERVIEW OF LIVER DISEASE MANAGEMENT PRINCIPLES
=============================================================

Management of liver disease follows these core principles:
1. Treat the underlying cause (etiology-specific therapy)
2. Prevent progression to cirrhosis
3. Monitor for and manage complications of cirrhosis
4. Prevent hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC)
5. Assess candidacy for liver transplant in advanced disease
6. Patient education and lifestyle modification

A multidisciplinary team approach is essential:
- Hepatologist/Gastroenterologist: Core specialist
- Primary care physician: Routine monitoring
- Dietitian/Nutritionist: Dietary management
- Pharmacist: Medication reconciliation (avoid hepatotoxic drugs)
- Social worker: Alcohol cessation support, transplant coordination
- Interventional radiologist: TIPS procedure
- Transplant surgeon: For advanced disease

SECTION 2: MANAGEMENT OF PORTAL HYPERTENSION
=============================================================

DEFINITION
Portal hypertension = portal venous pressure >5-6 mmHg above IVC pressure.
Clinically significant portal hypertension (CSPH) = hepatic venous pressure gradient (HVPG) ≥10 mmHg.
Clinical complications begin at HVPG ≥12 mmHg.

ESOPHAGEAL & GASTRIC VARICES

Screening and Surveillance:
- All newly diagnosed cirrhotic patients: Upper endoscopy (EGD)
- No varices found: Repeat EGD every 2-3 years (compensated cirrhosis)
- Small varices (<5mm): Repeat EGD every 1-2 years
- Large varices (>5mm): Treat prophylactically

Primary Prophylaxis (Preventing First Bleed):
Option 1 — Non-selective beta-blockers (NSBBs):
- Propranolol: 20-40mg twice daily (titrate to reduce heart rate 25%)
- Nadolol: 40-80mg once daily
- Carvedilol: 6.25-12.5mg once daily (most evidence; also reduces CSPH)
- Goal: Reduce heart rate by 25% OR to 55-60 bpm

Option 2 — Endoscopic variceal ligation (EVL):
- Performed every 2-4 weeks until varices obliterated
- Preferred when beta-blockers contraindicated or not tolerated
- Equally effective as NSBBs for large varices

Combination (NSBBs + EVL): Recommended for secondary prophylaxis (after first bleed)

Acute Variceal Bleeding (Emergency Management):
1. ABC: Airway, IV access, blood type and cross-match
2. Fluid resuscitation: Target Hb 7-8 g/dL (restrictive transfusion strategy)
3. Vasoactive drugs (start immediately, before endoscopy):
   - Terlipressin: 2mg IV every 4 hours (most evidence; reduces mortality)
   - Octreotide: 50mcg bolus, then 50mcg/hour infusion
   - Somatostatin: 250mcg bolus, then 250mcg/hour
4. Prophylactic antibiotics (mandatory):
   - Ceftriaxone: 1g IV daily × 7 days (preferred)
   - OR Norfloxacin: 400mg twice daily × 7 days
5. Endoscopy within 12 hours:
   - Endoscopic variceal ligation (EVL): Treatment of choice
   - Injection sclerotherapy: Alternative if EVL not possible
6. Rescue therapy for refractory bleeding:
   - TIPS (Transjugular Intrahepatic Portosystemic Shunt)
   - Balloon tamponade (Sengstaken-Blakemore tube): Temporary bridge to TIPS

Secondary Prophylaxis (Preventing Rebleed):
- Combination of NSBBs + EVL (superior to either alone)
- TIPS: For refractory varices or failure of pharmacological/endoscopic therapy
- Long-term NSBBs indefinitely

TIPS (Transjugular Intrahepatic Portosystemic Shunt)
- Interventional radiology procedure creating a shunt between portal and hepatic veins
- Rapidly decompresses portal pressure
- Indications:
  - Refractory variceal bleeding
  - Refractory ascites
  - Hepatic hydrothorax
  - Hepatorenal syndrome (as bridge to transplant)
- Complications:
  - Hepatic encephalopathy (major concern — occurs in 25-35%)
  - Shunt stenosis/occlusion (use PTFE-covered stents to reduce)
  - Worsening liver failure
- Contraindications: Severe hepatic encephalopathy, severe right heart failure, rapid progressive liver failure

SECTION 3: MANAGEMENT OF ASCITES
=============================================================

DEFINITION & GRADING
Ascites = fluid accumulation in the peritoneal cavity.
- Grade 1: Mild ascites detectable only by ultrasound
- Grade 2: Moderate ascites; abdomen distended
- Grade 3: Tense ascites; severely distended abdomen

FIRST-LINE TREATMENT
1. Sodium restriction: <2g/day (88 mmol/day) — crucial
2. Diuretics:
   - Spironolactone (aldosterone antagonist): Start 100mg/day, max 400mg/day
   - Furosemide (loop diuretic): Start 40mg/day, max 160mg/day
   - Ratio: 100mg spironolactone : 40mg furosemide (to maintain normokalemia)
   - Monitor: Electrolytes, renal function (creatinine), body weight (lose max 0.5kg/day without edema, 1kg/day with edema)
3. Avoid NSAIDs (worsen renal function, reduce diuretic efficacy)
4. Avoid ACE inhibitors/ARBs (risk of renal failure in cirrhosis)

THERAPEUTIC PARACENTESIS
Indications:
- Tense (Grade 3) ascites
- Diuretic-refractory ascites
- Respiratory distress from tense ascites

Procedure:
- Remove ascitic fluid via needle/catheter in left iliac fossa
- Large volume paracentesis (LVP): Remove >5L per session

Albumin infusion (mandatory with LVP):
- 8g IV albumin per liter of ascitic fluid removed >5L
- Prevents post-paracentesis circulatory dysfunction (PPCD)
- Reduces risk of renal failure, hepatorenal syndrome, and death

REFRACTORY ASCITES
Definition: Ascites not responding to maximum diuretics (spironolactone 400mg + furosemide 160mg) or diuretic intolerance.
Occurs in 5-10% of cirrhotic ascites patients.

Management:
1. Repeated large volume paracentesis + albumin (every 2-4 weeks)
2. TIPS: Reduces need for paracentesis; improves survival in selected patients
3. Liver transplant evaluation: Priority listing

SPONTANEOUS BACTERIAL PERITONITIS (SBP)
Diagnosis:
- Ascitic fluid PMN >250 cells/μL (neutrophil count)
- Culture often positive (E. coli, Klebsiella, Streptococcus)

Treatment:
- IV cefotaxime 2g every 8 hours × 5 days (or until PMN <250)
- IV albumin 1.5 g/kg on day 1, then 1g/kg on day 3 (prevents renal failure, improves survival)
- Response check: Repeat paracentesis at 48 hours

Primary SBP Prophylaxis (high-risk patients):
- Norfloxacin 400mg daily (in patients with ascitic protein <1.5g/dL + renal impairment OR Child-Pugh ≥9 + bilirubin ≥3)
Secondary SBP Prophylaxis (after first episode):
- Norfloxacin 400mg daily indefinitely OR until ascites resolved

HEPATIC HYDROTHORAX
- Pleural effusion (usually right-sided) from passage of ascites through diaphragmatic defects
- Management: Sodium restriction, diuretics; therapeutic thoracentesis; TIPS; avoid chest tube (high complication risk)

SECTION 4: MANAGEMENT OF HEPATIC ENCEPHALOPATHY (HE)
=============================================================

PATHOPHYSIOLOGY
Accumulation of toxins (primarily ammonia) due to liver failure and portosystemic shunting → cerebral dysfunction.
Ammonia levels do NOT perfectly correlate with HE grade.

GRADING (West Haven Criteria)
- Grade 0 (Covert HE): Subclinical; only detectable with neuropsychological testing
- Grade 1: Mild confusion, altered sleep, shortened attention span
- Grade 2: Lethargy, disorientation (especially time), asterixis (flapping tremor)
- Grade 3: Somnolence, gross disorientation, bizarre behavior, responsive to stimuli
- Grade 4: Coma, unresponsive

PRECIPITATING FACTORS (Must identify and treat)
- GI bleeding (most common acute precipitant)
- Infection (SBP, UTI, pneumonia)
- Constipation
- Dehydration/electrolyte imbalance (hyponatremia, hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia)
- Sedating medications (benzodiazepines, opioids, sleeping pills)
- Metabolic alkalosis
- Excessive dietary protein (rare with modern understanding)
- TIPS procedure (can precipitate chronic HE)
- Acute kidney injury/azotemia

ACUTE HE TREATMENT
1. Identify and treat precipitating cause (most important step)
2. Lactulose (first-line):
   - Oral: 15-30mL 2-4 times/day, titrate to 2-3 soft stools per day
   - Mechanism: Reduces intestinal pH → converts NH3 to NH4+ (non-absorbable) + cathartic effect
   - Enema: 300mL lactulose in 700mL water if unable to take orally
3. Rifaximin (adjunct or second-line):
   - 550mg orally twice daily
   - Non-absorbable antibiotic that reduces ammonia-producing gut bacteria
   - Superior to neomycin; minimal systemic side effects

SECONDARY PREVENTION (Preventing recurrence)
- Rifaximin 550mg twice daily + Lactulose: Most effective combination
- Reduces risk of overt HE recurrence by ~58%
- Continue indefinitely (high recurrence without treatment)

NUTRITION IN HEPATIC ENCEPHALOPATHY
- Adequate protein: 1.2-1.5g/kg/day (do NOT restrict protein — causes muscle wasting)
- Branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs): May benefit in refractory HE
- Small frequent meals; nighttime late snack to prevent fasting catabolism
- Zinc supplementation: 220mg zinc sulfate twice daily (zinc deficiency common in cirrhosis)

SECTION 5: MANAGEMENT OF HEPATORENAL SYNDROME (HRS)
=============================================================

DEFINITION
Functional acute kidney injury (AKI) in advanced cirrhosis caused by severe renal vasoconstriction.
- HRS-AKI (formerly HRS-1): Rapid (<48 hours) rise in creatinine; most severe; high mortality
- HRS-CKD (formerly HRS-2): Gradual progression; often associated with refractory ascites

DIAGNOSTIC CRITERIA (ICA-AKI 2019 Criteria)
- Cirrhosis with ascites
- Rise in serum creatinine >0.3 mg/dL within 48 hours OR 50% from baseline within 7 days
- No improvement after 48 hours of diuretic withdrawal + albumin challenge (1g/kg/day for 2 days)
- Absence of shock
- No current or recent nephrotoxic drug use
- No macroscopic signs of structural kidney injury

TREATMENT
First-line (if terlipressin available):
- Terlipressin: 1mg IV every 4-6 hours (titrate up to 2mg every 4 hours if no response)
- IV Albumin: 1g/kg on day 1, then 20-40g/day
- Goal: Creatinine reduction to <1.5 mg/dL (complete HRS reversal)
- Duration: Until reversal or maximum 14 days

Where terlipressin unavailable:
- Norepinephrine: 0.5-3mg/hour IV (ICU setting) + albumin
- Midodrine (7.5-12.5mg three times daily) + Octreotide (100-200mcg subcutaneously three times daily) + Albumin: Less effective but outpatient option

Renal Replacement Therapy (Dialysis):
- Supportive bridge to liver transplant; does not treat underlying cause

Liver Transplant:
- Definitive treatment for HRS
- Renal function improves after transplant in most patients
- Simultaneous liver-kidney transplant (SLK) if renal dysfunction persists

SECTION 6: HEPATOCELLULAR CARCINOMA (HCC) MANAGEMENT
=============================================================

STAGING — Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) System
| Stage | Description | Treatment |
|-------|-------------|-----------|
| BCLC 0 (Very early) | Single ≤2cm, PS 0, Child-Pugh A | Resection, ablation |
| BCLC A (Early) | Single ≤5cm or 2-3 ≤3cm, PS 0, Child-Pugh A-B | Resection, ablation, transplant |
| BCLC B (Intermediate) | Multi-nodular, PS 0, Child-Pugh A-B | TACE (Transarterial Chemoembolization) |
| BCLC C (Advanced) | Portal invasion, extrahepatic spread, PS 1-2 | Systemic therapy |
| BCLC D (End-stage) | Any PS 3-4, Child-Pugh C | Best supportive care |

CURATIVE TREATMENTS
Surgical Resection:
- Gold standard for early-stage HCC in non-cirrhotic liver
- 5-year survival: 40-70%
- Requires adequate liver reserve (Child-Pugh A, future liver remnant ≥20%)

Liver Transplant:
- Milan Criteria: Single lesion ≤5cm OR 2-3 lesions each ≤3cm, no vascular invasion, no extrahepatic disease
- 5-year survival: 65-70%
- Eliminates HCC + treats underlying cirrhosis

Local Ablation:
- Radiofrequency ablation (RFA): Thermal ablation; best for ≤3cm tumors
- Microwave ablation (MWA): Faster heating; effective for larger tumors
- Percutaneous ethanol injection (PEI): Older technique; for small lesions

PALLIATIVE TREATMENTS
Transarterial Chemoembolization (TACE):
- Delivers chemotherapy (doxorubicin, cisplatin) + embolic agent directly to tumor via hepatic artery
- Standard TACE: Conventional; Drug-eluting bead TACE (DEB-TACE): Newer, reduced systemic toxicity
- Indicated for intermediate-stage (BCLC B) HCC

Transarterial Radioembolization (TARE/Y-90):
- Yttrium-90 microspheres delivered via hepatic artery
- Delivers internal radiation to tumor
- Useful for portal vein invasion where TACE not suitable

SYSTEMIC THERAPY (Advanced HCC — BCLC C)
First-line:
- Atezolizumab + Bevacizumab (Tecentriq + Avastin): Preferred first-line; immunotherapy + anti-VEGF
- Durvalumab + Tremelimumab: Alternative first-line immunotherapy combination
- Sorafenib (Nexavar): Tyrosine kinase inhibitor; first systemic approval for HCC (2007)
- Lenvatinib (Lenvima): Non-inferior to sorafenib; alternative first-line

Second-line (after sorafenib failure):
- Regorafenib, Cabozantinib, Ramucirumab (AFP ≥400 ng/mL)

HCC SURVEILLANCE
- Ultrasound abdomen + serum AFP: Every 6 months for all cirrhotic patients
- High-risk non-cirrhotic HBV: Annual or biannual ultrasound
- Any liver nodule >1cm on ultrasound: Proceed to triphasic CT or MRI for characterization

SECTION 7: NUTRITION IN LIVER DISEASE
=============================================================

MALNUTRITION & SARCOPENIA
- Malnutrition affects 50-90% of patients with cirrhosis
- Sarcopenia (muscle wasting): Independent predictor of morbidity and mortality
- Leads to worse outcomes: Higher infection risk, worse hepatic encephalopathy, worse post-transplant survival

NUTRITIONAL REQUIREMENTS IN CIRRHOSIS
Energy: 35-40 kcal/kg/day (increased need due to hypermetabolic state)
Protein: 1.2-1.5g/kg/day (do NOT restrict unless absolutely necessary)
Sodium: <2g/day (88 mmol/day) to control ascites
Fluid restriction: Only if severe hyponatremia (Na <125 mmol/L)

SPECIFIC DIETARY ADVICE
- Do NOT restrict protein to treat hepatic encephalopathy (outdated practice)
- Small, frequent meals (4-6 per day) including late-night snack
- Late-night carbohydrate snack (50g) — reduces overnight fasting catabolism
- Branched-chain amino acids (BCAA) supplementation: May improve HE and nutritional status
- Zinc: Often deficient in cirrhosis; supplement 220mg zinc sulfate twice daily
- Vitamin D: Common deficiency; supplement if deficient
- Fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K): May need supplementation in cholestatic disease
- Avoid raw shellfish (Vibrio vulnificus risk); ensure proper food hygiene

DIET FOR NAFLD/MASLD
- Mediterranean diet: Best evidence-based diet for liver health
- Fructose restriction: Avoid high-fructose corn syrup, sugary beverages
- Coffee: 2-3 cups/day may be hepatoprotective
- Alcohol: Complete abstinence

SECTION 8: LIVER TRANSPLANT
=============================================================

INDICATIONS
- Decompensated cirrhosis (Child-Pugh C or MELD ≥15)
- Acute liver failure
- HCC within Milan criteria
- Metabolic liver diseases (Wilson disease, A1AT deficiency, glycogen storage diseases)
- Polycystic liver disease
- Primary sclerosing cholangitis

MELD SCORE AND TRANSPLANT PRIORITY
- Organs are allocated primarily by MELD score (severity of disease)
- MELD-Na (includes sodium): More accurate predictor of 90-day mortality
- Exception points: Awarded for HCC and other conditions not well captured by MELD

TYPES OF LIVER TRANSPLANT
1. Deceased donor transplant (DDLT): Most common; brain-dead or cardiac-dead donors
2. Living donor transplant (LDLT): Right lobe from living donor; growing availability
3. Split liver transplant: One donor liver split between two recipients (usually adult + child)

CONTRAINDICATIONS (Absolute)
- Active extrahepatic malignancy
- Uncontrolled sepsis outside hepatobiliary system
- Severe irreversible cardiopulmonary disease
- Active substance abuse (usually required 6-month sobriety)
- Hepatocellular carcinoma outside Milan criteria (without downstaging)

OUTCOMES
- 1-year survival: ~85-90%
- 5-year survival: ~70-75%
- 10-year survival: ~60-65%
- Recurrence of disease in transplanted liver: Common for HCV (now prevented with DAAs), NAFLD (recurs if metabolic risk not controlled), autoimmune hepatitis (immunosuppression protects), alcoholic cirrhosis (requires abstinence)

POST-TRANSPLANT CARE
- Lifelong immunosuppression (tacrolimus-based regimens)
- Regular monitoring: LFTs, tacrolimus levels, kidney function
- Infection prophylaxis (bacterial, fungal, viral — especially CMV)
- Screening for post-transplant malignancy (skin cancer, lymphoma)
- Management of metabolic complications (hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, obesity)

SECTION 9: MEDICATIONS TO AVOID IN LIVER DISEASE
=============================================================

HEPATOTOXIC MEDICATIONS (Dose-dependent)
- Acetaminophen/Paracetamol: Safe at reduced doses (<2g/day in mild liver disease; AVOID in moderate-severe); MOST COMMON cause of acute liver failure in the US/UK
- NSAIDs (ibuprofen, naproxen): Avoid — worsen renal function, promote GI bleeding
- Aspirin: Avoid (platelet inhibition + GI bleeding risk)

DRUGS CONTRAINDICATED IN LIVER DISEASE
- Methotrexate: Hepatotoxic; avoid in significant liver disease
- Isoniazid: Hepatotoxic; monitor closely
- Amiodarone: Phospholipidosis, hepatitis, cirrhosis
- Nitrofurantoin: Chronic use causes liver fibrosis
- Valproic acid: Hepatotoxic especially in children
- Phenytoin: Drug-induced liver injury
- Halothane (anesthetic): Avoid

DOSE ADJUSTMENT REQUIRED
- Many drugs require dose reduction in liver disease
- Child-Pugh score used to guide dosing
- Sedatives, opioids, anxiolytics: Greatly increased sensitivity; use with extreme caution
- Statins: Generally safe in liver disease; monitor LFTs

HERBAL SUPPLEMENTS (Often hepatotoxic)
- Kava kava: Severe liver toxicity reported
- Pennyroyal oil: Acute liver failure
- Germander: Hepatitis and liver failure
- Comfrey: Hepatic veno-occlusive disease
- Green tea extract (high-dose): Liver damage reported
- Pyrrolizidine alkaloids: Sinusoidal obstruction syndrome

SAFE ALTERNATIVES FOR PAIN
- Acetaminophen ≤2g/day: Safest analgesic in mild-moderate liver disease
- Tramadol: Use with caution (reduced dose)
- Consider non-pharmacological pain management

SECTION 10: LIVER DISEASE SCREENING & PREVENTION GUIDELINES
=============================================================

WHO SHOULD BE SCREENED FOR LIVER DISEASE?
High-risk populations:
- Heavy alcohol users: ALT, AST, GGT; FIB-4 annually
- Obese patients (BMI >30): ALT, FIB-4; ultrasound if elevated
- Type 2 diabetes patients: Annual ALT, FIB-4
- Patients on hepatotoxic medications: Regular LFTs per drug guidelines
- HIV-positive patients: Annual hepatitis B and C testing; LFTs
- IV drug users: Annual hepatitis B and C testing
- Blood transfusion before 1992: One-time HCV screening
- First-generation immigrants from HBV-endemic areas: HBsAg screening

VACCINATION RECOMMENDATIONS
Hepatitis A vaccine:
- All children at 12-23 months (2 doses)
- All adults ≥18 without prior immunity wanting protection
- All liver disease patients (including all chronic liver disease)
- Travelers to endemic regions
- MSM, IV drug users

Hepatitis B vaccine:
- All infants (at birth, then 2-month series)
- All adults through age 59 not previously vaccinated
- Adults ≥60: Based on risk factors and preference
- All liver disease patients not previously vaccinated
- Healthcare workers, dialysis patients, IV drug users, MSM, sexual partners of HBsAg+

KEY PREVENTION MESSAGES
- Limit alcohol: <14 units/week for men, <7 units/week for women (ideally abstain if liver disease)
- Maintain healthy weight and exercise regularly
- Avoid sharing needles or personal hygiene items
- Use condoms (reduce hepatitis B and C sexual transmission)
- Get vaccinated against hepatitis A and B
- Inform your doctor of ALL medications, supplements, and herbal remedies
- Avoid self-medication with hepatotoxic drugs
- Control blood sugar, blood pressure, and cholesterol
